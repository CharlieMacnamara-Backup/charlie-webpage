faff. I wanted better.

I use a hybrid: biga + starter. Big flavour, shorter proofs, flexible timing.
Bake by feel—no scales. Did I invent it? Doubt it. It makes great bread.

Don’t worry about the timeline—about five active minutes a day.

<TableOfContents />

Why this method works:

1. Long, slow fermentation (up to 36h) builds layered flavour
2. Parallel ferments (biga + starter) create complementary profiles
3. Flexible timing

You'll need:

#### Ingredients

- Unrefined sea salt
- Bread flour (1.5kg bag)
- Whole-grain flour
- Active sourdough starter
- Water
- Diastatic malt powder
- Brown sugar or natural sweetener (honey, malt syrup, maple…)

#### Equipment

- Large mixing container with lid
- Measuring cup for water
- Danish dough whisk
- Baking steel or stone
- Two shallow trays
- Sharp blade or lame

This recipe assumes a 1.5kg bag of strong white bread flour (common in
Scotland). I buy 20kg in bulk and measure by cup, but the small bag keeps the
recipe straightforward.

Diastatic malt boosts yeast activity. Worth it.

- 450g water (60% hydration)

### Building Strength Through Folding

Light kneading—consistency beats technique.

Start with stretch-and-folds: lift an edge until nearly tearing, fold to centre,
rotate 90°, repeat.

<SingleImage
  src="/blog/sourdough-journey/day2/unhydrated-flour.png"
  alt="Unhydrated flour clumps in dough"
  orientation="horizontal"
  blogSlug="sourdough-journey"
  objectFit="contain"
  aspectRatio="auto"
/>

This is what happens when you don't fold enough. Those pale patches show
unhydrated flour — dry pockets that never develop structure. A couple of rounds
of stretch-and-folds and the dough becomes one cohesive mass. Skip them and your
crumb will be dense and uneven where those clusters sat.

After 30 minutes, switch to coil folds: slide wet hands under the centre, lift,
tuck edges underneath.

### Final Shaping: Building Surface Tension

Dust bannetons with rice flour so gluten doesn’t stick.

Create tension without degassing:

1. Flip a round onto a lightly floured surface
2. Fold the far edge two-thirds toward you
3. Fold the near edge over that
4. Rotate 90° and repeat
5. Flip seam-side down
6. Drag toward you to tighten
7. Rotate a quarter turn and repeat

## Day Three: The End

Your dough has flavour and strength. Time to bake.

### Creating Your Baking Environment

Skip single-purpose kit. Use the open-bake method: Dutch oven results, home-oven
setup.

Set it up:

1. Baking steel on the middle rack
2. Steam pan on the bottom rack
3. Inverted tray on the top rack to trap steam
4. Heat to 250°C/480°F
5. Preheat 40 minutes for full saturation

Before baking, gather:

- Blade or lame
- Peel or board
- Heat-resistant gloves
- Spray bottle of water
- Kettle with boiling water

### Scoring

A well-placed score controls expansion and makes the ear.

  src="/blog/sourdough-journey/day3/angle.png"

For clean scores:

- Hold the blade at ~45°
- Make one confident ¼-inch cut
- Score right before loading
- Keep decorative cuts shallow (⅛-inch)

### The Bake

Move fast:

1. Load the loaf onto the hot steel
2. Pour boiling water into the steam pan
3. Mist the oven
4. Close the door

Two phases:

1. **Steam (30 minutes)**
   - Steam delays crust, maximizes expansion
   - Hold 250°C/480°F
2. **Crust (15–20 minutes)**
   - Remove steam pan and inverted tray (watch your hands)
   - Drop to 230°C/450°F
   - Rotate loaf 180°
   - Aim for deep caramelisation, not burnt

### The Result

You’re looking for:

- Deep mahogany crust
- A pronounced ear
- Hollow sound when tapped underneath

improve as it cools.

### The Journey Continues

This method will evolve, but the principles stay: patient fermentation, gentle
handling, attentive baking. As you gain confidence, tweak hydration, flour
blends, or add seeds. There’s no single right way—bread is oddly personal.
